\subsection{Automated Optimization of Approximate Accelerators}

Automated approximate-accelerator design determines how approximation should be introduced while balancing hardware efficiency and application quality.
Component libraries, including EvoApprox8b~\cite{mrazek2017evoapprox} and SMApproxLib~\cite{ullah2018smapproxlib}, provide arithmetic implementations with different error and hardware characteristics.
At the accelerator level, the optimization must decide where approximation is acceptable and how the selected implementations interact within the complete computation.
ABACUS~\cite{nepal2014abacus} explores behavioral approximation transformations, whereas library-based methods search over assignments of approximate arithmetic components.
AutoAx~\cite{mrazek2019autoax} combines learned cost and quality predictors with automated configuration search.
AxHLS~\cite{castrogodinez2020axhls} integrates approximate functional units into high-level synthesis (HLS), using analytical models to estimate resources and accuracy.
ApproxFPGAs~\cite{prabakaran2020approxfpgas} investigates the use of ASIC-oriented approximate components in FPGA systems, while Xel-FPGAs~\cite{prabakaran2023xelfpgas} adopts a multistage flow from arithmetic components to approximate functional modules and accelerator optimization.

Learning-based frameworks further exploit circuit structure.
ApproxPilot~\cite{zhang2025approxpilot} uses accelerator graphs to predict PPA and application quality and incorporates critical-path supervision into delay modeling.
ApproxGNN~\cite{vlcek2025approxgnn} learns gate-level component embeddings and transfers predictive models across related accelerator structures.
These approaches address complementary aspects of automated approximation: design transformation, component assignment, synthesis integration, structural prediction, and representation reuse.
However, a larger component-assignment space increases the difficulty of identifying useful PPA--quality tradeoffs, and predictive evaluation still depends on the training data available for the target problem.
The relevant question for this work is how measured configurations can support both reliable exploration and continued model improvement under a limited evaluation budget.

\subsection{Surrogate-Assisted Design Space Exploration}

Surrogate-assisted DSE reduces evaluation cost by using predictive models to rank candidate designs and identify configurations for expensive evaluation.
In a train-then-search workflow, a model is constructed before the main optimization and subsequently used to score a much larger set of candidates, as in AutoAx and ApproxPilot~\cite{mrazek2019autoax,zhang2025approxpilot}.
This organization makes search efficient, but its decisions depend on the coverage and accuracy of the initial model.
When the search concentrates on regions poorly represented in the training data, prediction errors can distort the apparent Pareto front and the selection of promising designs.

Sequential optimization instead alternates candidate selection, real evaluation, and surrogate updates.
ParEGO~\cite{knowles2006parego} uses scalarization and expected improvement for expensive multiobjective optimization, while HyperMapper~\cite{nardi2019hypermapper} combines objective regression with feasibility classification for constrained design spaces.
TuRBO~\cite{eriksson2019turbo} uses local models and adaptive trust regions to concentrate evaluations.
For hardware DSE, Reagen et al.~\cite{reagen2017bayesopt} apply Bayesian optimization to accelerator design, and BOOM-Explorer~\cite{bai2021boomexplorer} combines active learning with multiobjective Bayesian optimization for processor microarchitectures.
ARS-Flow and ARS-Flow 2.0~\cite{huang2024arsflow,huang2025arsflow2} use active learning and Pareto-region resampling for accelerator-rich systems.
Together, these studies motivate selecting measurements according to optimization progress rather than relying exclusively on a fixed initial dataset.

The surrogate implementation provides another important distinction.
AutoAx uses random forests over engineered component features~\cite{mrazek2019autoax}, offering inexpensive configuration-level prediction whose expressiveness depends on the structural and error information encoded in the inputs.
Gaussian-process models support acquisition decisions through predictive distributions~\cite{knowles2006parego,eriksson2019turbo,bai2021boomexplorer}, but their suitability depends on the design representation and covariance assumptions.
GNNs explicitly incorporate circuit connectivity.
ApproxPilot~\cite{zhang2025approxpilot} combines arithmetic-unit properties and error descriptors with accelerator topology and uses critical-path supervision for delay prediction.
ApproxGNN~\cite{vlcek2025approxgnn} learns gate-level component embeddings for transfer across related accelerator structures.
Such structural representations capture interactions beyond aggregate component statistics, while their predictive accuracy still depends on the available labeled designs and the relationship between training and target configurations.

Hardware-cost predictors illustrate how these representations can be tailored to different objectives.
Graph-based models have been developed for HLS performance~\cite{wu2022hls_gnn}, RTL-level PPA~\cite{xu2022sns}, and FPGA power estimation~\cite{lin2022powergear}.
Operation-delay and pre-routing timing models incorporate dependencies relevant to signal propagation~\cite{ustun2020delay,guo2022timingpredict}, while arithmetic-error features support application-accuracy prediction~\cite{mo2024errorfeatures}.
These studies reduce repeated synthesis and implementation effort, but accurate prediction alone is a different objective from identifying the best hardware--quality tradeoffs with a limited measurement budget.

Model adaptation and calibration address discrepancies between predictions and target measurements.
ApproxGNN investigates target-domain fine-tuning and a global additive correction for transferred quality predictions~\cite{vlcek2025approxgnn}.
Fine-tuning adapts the learned mapping using target labels, whereas a uniform additive correction compensates for a shared shift without changing pairwise prediction differences.
This distinction matters for DSE because candidate ranking depends on relative predictions, and prediction errors need not be uniform across the explored configurations.

Table~\ref{tab:method_comparison} summarizes these complementary capabilities.
AutoAx provides feature-based cost and quality prediction; ApproxPilot adds circuit and path information; ApproxGNN supports transferable representations and calibration; and ARS-Flow 2.0 incorporates model updates during optimization.
For the approximate-accelerator methods compared here, structural modeling and transfer improve predictive capability, but do not by themselves ensure reliable search with sparse target labels or adapt predictions to newly explored regions.
Sequential hardware DSE provides useful sampling and update strategies, while leaving the question of how best to combine them with configuration-dependent correction for joint PPA and application-quality optimization.

These observations motivate ApproxPilot-SE: a framework that supports exploration from limited initial data by combining similar-configuration retrieval and local latency calibration with Pareto-guided evaluation and iterative surrogate training.
The same new measurements provide calibration references and training labels, coupling correction of current predictions with refinement of subsequent search models.
